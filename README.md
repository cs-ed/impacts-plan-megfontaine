# Impacts on Society

### Course
#### AP Computer Science Principles
A Computer Science course designed to introduce students to fundamental computing concepts and practices.
Grades 10-12

#### Summary
Exploring the Impacts of Computing is one of the five “Big Ideas” of the AP Computer Science Principles curriculum. This portion of the course investigates benefits and harms of computing innovations, the digital divide, computing bias, crowdsourcing, legal concerns, ethical concerns, and safe computing. This plan outlines a series of learning activities designed to support students in an engaging exploration of these topics.

## Plan

### Learning Objectives

* Students will correctly define the following terms: digital divide, computing bias, crowdsourcing, phishing, malware, rogue access point, DDoS, encryption, decryption
* Students will identify and explain ethical considerations related to the use of technology
* Students will identify and explain examples of algorithmic bias in computing contexts
* Students will identify and explain potential benefits and harms of computing technologies
* Students will analyze their own values and beliefs as users and creators of technology in order to clearly articulate their personal perspective on the impacts of computing


### Learning Activities

#### Length of Unit
9 Block Periods
#### Lesson Structure
The first 7 block days of this unit will follow a standard structure. Each day will begin with a current events journal activity, followed by teacher-lead direct instruction, and conclude with an independent homework assignment. The last 2 block periods will be dedicated to a unit review project. The unit will conclude with a project and a short multiple choice exam.

#### Current Events Journals
Students will be asked to find a story from a credible publication (print or online) that relates to the central topics covered in this unit. Topics may include: new computing innovations, legal or legislative issues related to tech and computing, big data collection/application, ethical issues related to computing, and online safety. The teacher will have a collection of articles available for students who have difficulty finding a source.

Students will then complete a journal entry answering the following questions about their story:
1. What is the issue/technology described?
2. Who is impacted? (Who is this tech designed for? Who benefits from this tech? Are there any potential harms?)
3. Where is the action occurring? (Online? In real life? In particular communities?)
4. When is this taking place? (Are the impacts immediate, have they already happened, or will they occur in the future?)
5. Why is this important? (Why should we pay attention to this issue?)

#### Direct Instruction
The direct instruction portion of each lesson will involve lecture, readings, and small group discussions. The instructor will present information on foundational concepts related to ethical computing and impacts of computing on society. Lectures will focus on engaging, real-world examples that illustrate the interplay between computing and society. Examples of positive and negative impacts of computing will be considered. Informal, small group discussions will punctuate lectures, providing opportunities for students to reflect on central themes and clarify their understanding with peer support. Reading selections will tie-in and extend the central themes of the lessons.

#### Homework
The format of homework assignments will vary. Students may be presented with an instructor-curated list of resources to read, then asked to respond to several reflection questions related to the central concepts of the unit. Students may be asked to consider a particular perspective/position connected to a societal impact of computing, and find/summarize a small collection of resources to support that perspective.


### Assessment

#### Formative Assessment
During the daily lessons, the teacher will circulate to address questions or provide clarification to students on their assignments. The teacher will listen to student discussion, interjecting to provide feedback as needed. Daily homework assignments will be graded based on effort, and during each class period some time will be devoted to addressing any common misconceptions or questions that arise each day.

#### Summative Assessments
A multiple choice exam, modeled after the AP Exam, will be administered at the end of the unit. This exam is designed to assess student understanding of key vocabulary terms and concepts established in the unit. Students will be asked to correctly define key vocabulary, identify central unit concepts. Some questions will also present a scenario related to an impact of computing, and ask students to apply their understanding of key concepts to select the most appropriate answer. This assessment primarily measures success on the “define” and "identify" components of the unit learning goals. 

In addition to the multiple choice exam, students will be required to complete a summative assessment based upon the journaling activities assigned throughout the unit. Students will review their journal entries, and choose one topic of interest that they would like to explore further. Students will be asked to create a “1 Pager” related to their chosen topic. To complete the 1 Pager, students will identify 2-3 additional sources related to their chosen topic and its impacts on society. Then, they will create a concise summary of their topic through the lens of its ethical and social impacts. 

Upon completion of the 1 Pagers, the class will hold a Gallery Walk, where students circulate through the classroom, reviewing each other’s 1 Pager. Finally, students will be asked to respond, in writing, to the following reflection prompt: How will you consume, create, and use technology differently in the future based on the ideas you have learned in this unit? 

The 1 Pager and written reflection are designed to measure the “explain” and “analyze” components of the unit learning goals.


### Outcomes

This unit plan places the impacts of computing on society as its explicit, central focus. Lessons will endeavor to present a nuanced and varied picture of the interplay between technology and society, both past and present. Discussions and reflection assignments will ask students to think critically about the positive and negative consequences of technologies. Opportunities will arise to explore the reciprocal relationship between computing and society, and to examine the ways each influences the other. Varied learning modalities, including lecture, reading, writing, and discussion, will provide students with opportunities to understand central unit concepts from a variety of perspectives, deepening understanding. Students will also be encouraged to discover and reflect upon their own personal views of computing’s impacts, making connections that support learning and retention.

The deliberate inclusion of varied sources of information representing a broad spectrum of identities and perspectives will serve to center diversity, equity, and inclusion within the unit. The instructor will ensure that examples and resources provided highlight different ways computing has positively and negatively impacted multiple different ethnic, racial, and cultural subgroups. Through the journaling activities, students will be encouraged to explore their own personal interests and perspectives. This will ensure that each students’ personal experiences will be represented in the learning experience.
